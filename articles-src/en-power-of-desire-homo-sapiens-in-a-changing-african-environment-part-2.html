Homo sapiens in a Changing African Environment (continued)

After leaving Africa, Homo sapiens met Neanderthals and Denisovans — how did tools, language, and storytelling change the way humans lived?

Jielin Dong

As Homo sapiens spread into Eurasia, they encountered Neanderthals, Denisovans, and other archaic humans, and interbreeding occurred. Neanderthals disappeared about forty thousand years ago, but some of their genetic inheritance survives in people today. Their rise and disappearance involved environmental conditions, population size, group connections, and competition for resources. The details remain under investigation.

Africa preserves important evidence of early technological development, while archaic humans in Eurasia also created and improved tools. Discoveries of Neanderthal composite tools and plant-fiber processing reveal their technical abilities. Migrating populations carried knowledge, creating opportunities for further experimentation and exchange.

Most of human technological history belongs to the Paleolithic. Durable stone tools provide important clues to this vast period. They could cut, scrape, pound, and assist hunting. Oldowan tools, Acheulean hand axes, and later prepared-core techniques reveal different methods of production. Their dates and distributions overlap, and different regions followed their own trajectories.

Wooden artifacts survive less readily than stone, making the few examples from hundreds of thousands of years ago especially valuable. Wooden structural elements from Kalambo Falls in Zambia, dating to approximately 476,000 years ago, show that archaic humans could work and join timber. Wooden hunting implements in Europe provide another kind of evidence. These discoveries expand our understanding of our ancestors’ ability to work materials.

Researchers have interpreted some stone points from Kathu Pan in South Africa, approximately five hundred thousand years old, as spear tips mounted on wooden shafts. Hafting changes how a tool is held and used and how forces act on it. Composite tools require their makers to connect different materials in the mind before assigning each a function: Stone supplies the cutting edge, wood extends the arm, and together they create capabilities neither offers alone. This is a cognitive leap worth imagining. From tying a stone to a shaft to assembling thousands of components into a machine, humans have continually developed this way of thinking through combinations.

Homo sapiens inherited and improved many technologies as they dispersed. Skills varied among groups according to environment, experience, and exchange. These tools look simple today, but producing them required repeated observation and experiment. Creating an effective tool and passing on the skill to make it were both demanding achievements. The few surviving artifacts give us glimpses of that long accumulation.

Clothing, fishing implements, and more refined stone tools expanded ways of obtaining food and resisting the cold. Barbed bone points from Katanda in the Democratic Republic of the Congo are approximately eighty to ninety thousand years old. Small-blade technology in South Africa around seventy-one thousand years ago could be used in composite tools. Some points from South Africa dating to about sixty-four thousand years ago also offer clues to early archery.

Language enables people to share experience, coordinate action, and transmit knowledge. Its origins cannot be reconstructed directly from artifacts. Researchers must draw on anatomy, genetics, and behavioral evidence. Shared stories and collective imagination may also have helped people cooperate on a larger scale.

Ornaments, pigments, and engravings show our ancestors’ attempts to express identity and meaning. They may also have projected human experience onto nature: Sky and earth, mountains and rivers, and animals might possess intentions and emotions, could be addressed, and might even be bargained with. Such animistic imagination began to give a world full of danger and chance a set of understandable relationships.

Following this reasoning, people might try to deal with the natural world as they dealt with one another: through imitation, appeasement, intimidation, and gifts, using rituals to seek game, rain, or safety. This was a bold intellectual departure. The senses reach what is immediately present; imagination can connect unseen powers and construct an account of the world that guides action. Practical experience, aesthetics, stories, and belief could grow together in this space.

We can imagine gifted storytellers beside a fire beneath the stars, recounting the cleverness of animals, the fortunes of ancestors, and the temperaments of spirits. These keepers of memory and interpreters of the world may have been early shamans and knowledge workers. Stories gave communities a shared imagination of the past and future and traveled with successive generations of migrants. The beautiful, abundant Eden of later creation stories, lost and never to be regained, might echo a longing for a distant homeland—even preserve some indistinct memory of ancestral Africa.

The US-China AI Battle: Whose Dividend? Whose Price?

Companies, Countries, and Individuals Have Different Scoreboards, and They Are Not Commensurable

Jielin Dong

In the script of history, the triumph of technology is often folded into national glory and the market value of tech giants, while the price of transformation falls silently on the shoulders of every individual.

On July 24, 2026, an open letter titled "Open Weights and US AI Leadership" sparked a new round of debate in Silicon Valley. Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta, IBM, Palantir, Hugging Face, and a group of cloud computing, cybersecurity, and venture capital institutions jointly urged Washington not to prematurely restrict open-weight models that can be downloaded, modified, and autonomously deployed. The open letter described open models as the foundation of American innovation, competition, security, and technological sovereignty.

This letter did not directly name China, but it is impossible to read it without considering China. Over the past year, the capabilities and global adoption rate of China's open-weight models have risen rapidly, and Washington is also discussing whether to restrict or even ban Chinese models from entering the US market. Anthropic, which did not sign the open letter, has become the most prominent cautious faction: it emphasizes the biological and cybersecurity risks that frontier models may bring, advocates mandatory testing for highly capable models, and strengthening control over chips and model distillation.

On the surface, this is a governance debate about whether "open is safer"; in reality, it is also a debate over interests and industrial positioning. Nvidia and cloud platforms want models to proliferate widely to drive computing power and hosting demand; Meta hopes the model layer becomes a cheap complement to consolidate distribution, advertising, and application ecosystems; Harness and application companies want to retain the ability to replace underlying models while controlling customer data, processes, and entry points; companies relying on charging for closed-source models emphasize the risks of capability loss of control and irreversible proliferation. Developers, workers, consumers, and capital owners are also protecting their tools, jobs, choices, or returns on investment. This does not mean security concerns are fake. Once open weights are released, they are difficult to recall, and closed-source systems can also be attacked, abused, or fail under circumstances where outsiders cannot inspect them.

The grand narrative and the role of considering the overall interests of the United States belong to Washington. On the one hand, the White House defines "winning the AI race" as a goal for economic competitiveness and national security, and requires national security departments to simultaneously adopt the most advanced closed-source models and open technologies; on the other hand, it has established risk identification and pre-release communication mechanisms for frontier models, and retains the space to implement restrictions on the grounds of intellectual property, cybersecurity, and competition with China.

This debate reveals a frequently confused issue: what scoreboard should we use to understand the winners and losers of the US-China AI competition? Model rankings measure a type of technical product; companies care about moats and profit flows; governments care about technological leadership, industrial control, national security, social governance, and global standards; individuals care whether AI expands or compresses their income, opportunities, choices, security, and dignity. Therefore, when discussing the US-China AI dispute, we must simultaneously check the three scoreboards of companies, countries, and individuals, the results of which may have opposite signs to each other.

I. Dismantling the Six-Layer Ecosystem: Great Power Rivalry Goes Far Beyond Model Competition

AI is not just a model, but a set of interdependent ecosystems. Jensen Huang summarizes it into five layers: power, chips, infrastructure, large models, and applications. Chamath Palihapitiya further separates data and control (Harness), forming a six-layer structure (as shown in Figure 1). The relatively special Harness layer is responsible for coordinating the operation of different models and agents; if the model is a horse, Harness is the harness and reins that determine how it works. Technological frontiers and value accumulation are not necessarily located in the same layer: models determine the upper limit of intelligence supply, but durable customer lock-in may occur in the Harness and application layers. A major power's AI strength depends on whether the entire ecosystem is robust and forms a closed loop, rather than a single-point ranking in any layer.

Different layers provide different opportunities for capital, entrepreneurs, and individuals. Currently, the most active areas for entrepreneurship in Silicon Valley are concentrated in the relatively asset-light Harness layer and application layer, where entrepreneurs attempt to translate general intelligence into specific business value; the advantages of large capital are more reflected in capital-intensive areas such as infrastructure construction, continuous model training, and platform ecosystem expansion. A small number of top talents can earn high premiums in the chip, model, and platform layers due to their scarce capabilities. Meanwhile, although users enjoy the dividends brought by the popularization of AI capabilities and price drops, they may also pay the price in terms of personal data, attention, and platform dependence; as workers, they may also face risks of job replacement, income polarization, and decreased bargaining power.

Comparing layer by layer, the US and China currently have their own strengths: the US possesses the most advanced chips, the strongest frontier closed-source models, abundant private capital, and a high-quality enterprise software market; China has more abundant power, faster infrastructure construction, stronger manufacturing organizational capabilities, and has temporarily gained an advantage in the global proliferation of open-weight models. American workers gain access to the strongest tools earlier, but also bear the pressure of high-wage jobs being replaced earlier; Chinese workers use models more cheaply, but whether this translates into wages, entrepreneurship, and career advancement still depends on the market and system.

Recently, model capabilities are gradually converging, but the physical foundations supporting AI development in China and the US are not approaching synchronously. The core bottlenecks faced by the two countries are not located in the same layer. For China, the main constraints come from advanced AI chips—affected both by US export controls and the shortcomings of domestic chips in performance, production capacity, and supporting ecosystems, though China has stronger power generation and engineering construction capabilities. Although the US has relatively abundant high-end chips, it faces infrastructure constraints such as power supply, grid connection approvals, and data center construction.

These constraints at different levels will ultimately be reflected in the construction speed of AI data centers and the deployment scale of effective computing power. According to scenario estimations using a unified metric of peak FP16 computing power, in 2025, US AI computing power will be about 16 times that of China; this gap may expand to about 25 times by 2027. Long-term predictions carry significant uncertainty, but it remains a relatively robust judgment that the US will maintain a lead of about an order of magnitude over the next three to five years.

Constraints not only determine "how much can be done", but also change "how to choose". US companies tend to expand scale and build ultra-large clusters in environments with relatively sufficient computing power, but power and grid connections limit speed; Chinese companies develop efficiency routes such as distillation, sparsification, quantization, and inference optimization under chip constraints. Constraints are thus written into corporate business models and the solutions of a generation of engineers. Even if lifted in the future, path dependence may not be easily overcome.

The constraints of the two countries will also ultimately translate into personal costs: US benefits may be concentrated among a few technology and capital owners, while China's costs of redundant construction, adaptation, and catching up may be transferred to individuals through public capital, profit pressure, and labor intensity.

II. The Paradox of Open Weights: Distribution Advantage Does Not Equal Commercial Victory

Chinese large model manufacturers such as Qwen, DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, and Zhipu have rapidly entered the global developer community through open weights, low-cost APIs, and toolchain construction. Against the backdrop of converging model capabilities and continuously declining call prices, open weights lower the barrier for developers to try, modify, and autonomously deploy models, and have become China's most internationally influential competitive approach in AI currently.

Hugging Face reports show that over the past year, models originating from China accounted for 41% of model downloads on the platform; as of April 2026, cumulative downloads of the Qwen family approached one billion. On the call side, DeepSeek became the largest provider on OpenRouter with an approximately 16.3% Token share. Calibers of different platforms cannot be combined, but they collectively indicate that Chinese models have achieved a significant global distribution advantage. For China, open weights not only bring model adoption but also expand technological influence and strategic space.

But there is a paradox here: the overseas distribution and use of Chinese open models still heavily rely on US-dominated infrastructure. Weights propagate via Hugging Face, calls enter inference services via platforms like OpenRouter and Vercel, and the underlying layer generally runs on the NVIDIA system. Chinese companies provide models, while the US ecosystem provides developer entry points, computing power, cloud, routing, and payment channels; the greater the usage of Chinese models, the more it may increase the revenue of US infrastructure companies.

Note that downloads do not equal deployment, deployment does not equal continuous usage, and API volume does not equal revenue. Vercel data shows that Chinese open models account for about one-third of token usage on its gateway, but correspond to less than 4% of user spending. Open weights can exchange for developers, reputation, and ecosystem entry points, but they also weaken direct charging capabilities, making it easier for customers to copy, modify, or replace models.

Therefore, nations can gain influence from technology diffusion, while model companies bear the costs of training, toolchains, and low-cost APIs. A considerable portion of the value may flow to chips, cloud, inference, routing, as well as Harness and application companies. What China currently wins is a model distribution advantage, not the entire value chain; whether it can be transformed into corporate revenue and complete national technological capability depends on whether it can move from downloads to deployment, from usage to payment, and from productivity improvements to revenue distribution.

For users, open weights lower the barriers to learning, experimentation, and entrepreneurship, and reduce dependence on a single closed-source platform; but capability availability does not equate to income growth. Developers may still lack customers and distribution channels, workers may increase output without sharing in productivity dividends, and ordinary users may also bear risks of data leaks, scams, and model abuse.

III. When Token Prices Plummet, the Rules of Competition Will Be Rewritten

Over the past few years, the most significant change in the AI industry has not just been the improvement in model capabilities, but the rapid decline in the price of acquiring equivalent intelligence. In the past three years, the price of tokens required to reach similar capabilities has dropped by more than two orders of magnitude. If this trend continues, a technological lead of a few months will become increasingly difficult to automatically translate into lasting commercial value.

The drop in token prices is not entirely created by the model layer alone, but is a product of the collective progress of the entire ecosystem: continuous improvements in chip performance, cluster interconnectivity, and utilization rates; inference frameworks, quantization, sparsification, speculative decoding, caching, and routing reducing the computation required for inference; cloud platforms improving equipment utilization; and model architectures and training methods enabling smaller models to achieve capabilities previously only possible with large models. In recent years, the rate of decline in token prices for equivalent capabilities has been much faster than hardware performance improvements explained by Moore's Law.

Market competition has further accelerated price cuts. As more models achieve similar capabilities, manufacturers find it difficult to maintain high prices merely by being "smarter" and can only compete for users through price cuts, free tiers, and product bundling. Therefore, token price reduction is both a result of technological progress and competition, indicating that certain general capabilities are showing a commoditization trend; but if a difficult-to-replicate AGI-level breakthrough occurs, the scarcity and pricing power of the model layer may rise again.

At the end of July 2026, OpenAI made GPT-5.6 Luna the default free model for ChatGPT Free and Go users, and dropped API input and output prices to $0.2 and $1.2 per million tokens respectively, showing top US companies starting to use free products and massive price cuts to compete for mass users, developers, and global distribution entry points. At the same time, multiple Chinese model vendors announced price hikes, with DeepSeek's new prices taking effect on August 17.

Low-priced tokens do not equal low costs. It is necessary to distinguish between the token price paid by customers, the cost to achieve a certain model capability, and the unit effective computing cost to complete the equivalent training or inference workload. The first is affected by subsidies and competition; algorithmic optimization can lower the second, but does not necessarily make the underlying computing system cheaper. To measure the economic efficiency of underlying computing power, one should compare the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO), including chips and depreciation, power and cooling, interconnect and storage, data center facilities, software adaptation, and maintenance; then divide by the actual effective workload completed by the system to get a comparable unit effective computing cost.

Chinese AI companies have certain advantages in labor, engineering construction, and algorithm efficiency (such as distillation, quantization, sparsification, etc.), which can lower adaptation and maintenance expenses. But in key links of underlying effective computing power, domestic platforms still face insufficient single-card performance, chip interconnectivity, cluster stability, and software maturity, resulting in the need for more hardware, power, and engineering resources to complete equivalent tasks. Lower utilization rates also mean theoretical computing power cannot fully translate into usable throughput; if choosing NVIDIA platforms directly, they must face downgraded models, export licenses, and scarcity premiums. Because these overall disadvantages in hardware, software, and the ecosystem offset part of the efficiency advantages, some public system comparisons show that when completing equivalent effective training or inference workloads, the unit effective computing cost of domestic AI stacks may still be significantly higher than that of NVIDIA platforms.

The recent price increases by Chinese model companies are more likely a result of multiple combined factors: fading subsidies, rising training and service costs, capacity constraints, shareholders beginning to demand revenue and profits, and the full-stack TCO gradually returning to product pricing. The low prices of Chinese models are not entirely without an efficiency basis, but the magnitude of the price advantage exceeded the underlying cost advantage, and the difference had to be paid for by sacrificing profits and capital returns, making price hikes inevitable.

IV. Where Does Value Accumulate? A Reshuffling of Application Ecosystems, Markets, and Capital

Assuming no AGI-level breakthrough to reopen the model gap, new AI value may increasingly migrate to the Harness and application layers, which control customer data, business processes, specific scenarios, and distribution entry points. Harness and applications determine how capabilities enter businesses, reach users, and accumulate value; the market determines payment demand and revenue space; capital determines resource flows and corporate forms.

Harness and Applications Control Customer Stickiness and Entry Points

Harness is responsible for connecting models into enterprise data, permissions, software, and business processes, turning outputs into actionable results; applications package capabilities into products directly adoptable by users, commanding specific scenarios, interactions, distribution, and customer relationships. Enterprise data and processes are hard to migrate, and user habits, brands, and channels are not easily replicated, therefore even if underlying models can be swapped, Harness and applications may still form stable stickiness.

From a national competition perspective, Harness determines how intelligence enters the business processes of enterprises and public organizations, while applications determine who controls user entry points, use cases, and data feedback loops. Therefore, these two layers are critical for translating model capabilities into real productivity, economic value, and organizational capabilities, and are also important landing points for data control and data sovereignty.

For individuals, Harness and applications together decide whether AI is an assistant, a supervisor, or a replacement. Data permissions, performance metrics, and automation boundaries will alter employee autonomy and bargaining power; product design, recommendation logic, and business models will influence what tools individuals can use, how they are reached, and what price they pay. Whether individuals benefit depends not just on knowing how to use models, but on whether they can retain professional judgment, customer relationships, choices, and the right to revenue distribution.

Markets Determine Commercial Compounding and Monetization Potential

The market determines how much revenue AI can generate after deployment. The US has high enterprise software budgets, mature subscription habits, and expensive white-collar labor. As long as AI can replace some software functions, or reduce the work hours of programmers, analysts, lawyers, or customer service agents, enterprises have clear payment incentives; revenue can then support the next round of investment in computing power, R&D, and services, creating commercial compounding. This is the market foundation for Coding Agents becoming AI's "super application" in the US currently.

China possesses numerous application scenarios and strong deployment capabilities, but enterprise software budgets are typically lower, and intellectual labor costs are also relatively low. Currently, no super enterprise applications have emerged. If the cost of purchasing tokens, transforming software, and remodeling processes is higher than adding human labor, customers lack payment incentives. The government and enterprise market can provide early clients, but custom projects are hard to replicate at a low cost like standard software. The quality difference between US and China markets is also a key reason why Chinese model companies go overseas early. Additionally, the consumer side can rely on advertising, e-commerce, and traffic monetization—in the internet era, China's super apps appeared on the personal consumer end.

Corporate compounding does not equal personal compounding. Corporate adoption of AI may allow employees to increase output and income, but may also eliminate jobs, leaving productivity dividends mostly for shareholders. Whether individuals benefit depends on whether their skills are scarce, whether they own customer relationships or capital, and how new revenue is distributed.

Capital Determines the Direction of Corporate Growth

Funds for the US AI ecosystem primarily come from venture capital, public capital markets, and massive capital expenditures by super-tech companies, and their resource allocation typically favors the technological frontier, high growth, and globally scalable commercial revenues. Funds for Chinese AI enterprises come from national industrial funds, internet companies, local governments, and venture capital institutions; apart from commercial returns, some capital also shoulders objectives like technological autonomy, industrial localization, and building national strategic capabilities. Notably, the influence of government capital often goes beyond its direct shareholding ratio: subsidies, procurement, market access, policy signals, and follow-on financing can all magnify its impact on corporate decision-making. Different capital structures thus shape different corporate goals, and push companies toward different customers, products, and development paths. When model capabilities converge while training and service costs remain high, capital is more likely to flow to companies that control cloud, traffic, customers, data, Harness, and application entry points.

For individuals, capital allocation directions ultimately translate into career opportunities, income returns, and risks. The US system may create a small number of extremely high-return research, engineering, and entrepreneurial jobs, but also rapidly lay off, acquire, or consolidate when growth falls short of expectations, leading to highly concentrated and volatile individual opportunities. Employment opportunities in the Chinese system might be more concentrated in government/enterprise project implementation, industrial deployment, and localized execution, but the space for individuals to obtain excess returns and cross-market mobility is relatively limited, and career prospects are more easily influenced by policy directions, government procurement, and local investment cycles.

V. Future Industry Deductions: Three Predictions on Open Weights, Restructuring, and AI Borders

Prediction 1 | The US Open Weight Camp Expands, Triggering a Global Reshuffling of Token Shares

On August 10, 2026, Meta announced the open-weight release of its flagship Muse Spark 1.2 model, becoming a clear signal that the US is responding to the Chinese camp with high-quality open-weight models. What remains to be seen is how many top-tier models will follow suit, and who will capture more token distribution, developers, and ecosystem entry points after opening up.

This prediction includes three sub-judgments: First, more top-tier US models will open their weights, and drive a significant increase in the token share of US open models in major routers, cloud platforms, and AI gateways; Second, competition among Chinese open models like Qwen, DeepSeek, and Moonshot AI will intensify, while the direct commercial pressure on top US companies will mainly come from US models competing on low prices; Third, US models will still choose selective over comprehensive opening, and frontier capabilities with high biological, cyber, or autonomous agent risks may continue to remain behind closed-source controlled APIs.

Falsification Criteria: Within the next 18 months, the token share of US-origin open models on major global platforms does not significantly rise, or Chinese models visibly squeeze the revenues of US model companies.

Prediction 2 | The Model Layer Will First Undergo Two Years of Fierce Competition, Followed by an Integration Period

Over the next two years, major US and Chinese model companies will remain in a phase of fierce competition and continuous investment. By around late 2028, if no AGI breakthrough occurs that can restructure the competitive landscape, as model capabilities further converge and differentiation space shrinks, while training, inference, and iteration costs remain high, some companies lacking stable revenue, capital backing, or unique capabilities may struggle to survive. At that point, the model layers in both China and the US will likely see closures, mergers, and acquisitions.

If a company takes the lead in achieving a decisive AGI breakthrough, the scarcity of the model layer will rise again. In this case, integration will still occur, but will primarily manifest as capital, talent, and customers rapidly concentrating toward the technological leader, while stragglers are swiftly eliminated.

Falsification Criteria: Sometime after the end of 2028, no multiple verifiable model layer M&A or independent business terminations have occurred in either the US or Chinese ecosystems, and independent model companies still broadly rely on model revenue growth.

Prediction 3 | The Boundary Between China and the US in the Future AI World Will Resemble the Internet Territories

Today's internet territories are roughly divided into US platform-dominated zones, China's independent system, and mixed zones adopting technologies from both. The AI territory is very likely to replicate this map, and beyond model technology itself, chips, clouds, payments, app stores, data rules, government procurement, and security standards will jointly dictate technological affiliation.

This boundary will directly affect what tools individuals can use, whether data can cross borders, in which markets skills are transferable, and where startup products can be sold. In the short term, the US may not ban Chinese models, because the value they create through the catfish effect, open weights, local deployment, and vendor optionality may still outweigh market and security costs; but this tolerance is conditional. Once Chinese models cause visible security, industrial, media, or political problems, and the geopolitical faction gains the upper hand, policies could escalate from restriction to prohibition.

This dividing line is not formed through entirely free competition, and the hand of the government is never absent: the US emphasizes national security and market access, China focuses on model access and export, as well as content and social control, and Europe stresses risk classification. Although the regulatory directions differ, they collectively increase the institutional costs of cross-border deployment.

Falsification Criteria: If a third pole independent of the US emerges in places like Europe or India, or if the Chinese tech stack becomes dominant in several large economies where its internet never took hold, this judgment will need revision.

VI. The Ultimate Victory and the Inquiries of the Three Scoreboards

In the future, both China and the US are very likely to declare victory in the AI competition, but the structure of their victories will be starkly different. The US is more likely to define victory by capital returns, scientific discoveries, and the expansion of individual capabilities; China is more likely to define victory by industrial scale, technology diffusion, and the ability to shape national organizational capabilities and social order. American advantages more easily accumulate into quantifiable, compounding commercial wealth, whereas Chinese advantages manifest more as difficult-to-price state capabilities and strategic influence. The same technological revolution may reinforce the existing directions of the two systems, and thereby shape two different future societies.

However, regardless of which type of victory it is, the "three scoreboards" of companies, countries, and individuals influence one another but cannot substitute for one another. A US AI company might attain astonishing profits and market value, yet simultaneously replace a large number of white-collar jobs and concentrate productivity dividends; China might leverage AI to enhance manufacturing and governance capabilities, but the employment, income, career security, and autonomy of many ordinary people may not improve in tandem, and they might even become the price paid. If the company wins, the country does not necessarily win; if the country wins, ordinary people do not necessarily win.

More importantly: What kind of future will the two countries respectively build with AI? Who will reap the productivity dividends? Who will bear the technological risks and consequences? Does the enhancement of national power simultaneously expand the opportunities, choices, and dignity of ordinary people?

In this sweeping wave of AI, everyone is not merely a spectator or cheerleader, but a participant, and should constantly ask themselves: Am I a winner?

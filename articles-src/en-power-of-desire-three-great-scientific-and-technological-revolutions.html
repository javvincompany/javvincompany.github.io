Three Great Scientific and Technological Revolutions

Subsistence, communication, efficiency — how did three great revolutions change human society?

Jielin Dong

Looking back over more than ten thousand years through this classification, we can discern three great revolutions that transformed the foundations of human life. The first made food supplies more dependable. The second enabled information to be preserved, transmitted, and accumulated. The third vastly expanded humanity’s ability to use resources and create wealth. The author calls them the Subsistence Revolution, the Communication Revolution, and the Efficiency Revolution. Each corresponds to the main direction of one of the periods of intense innovation described above, and each continues to shape subsequent change.

The Subsistence Revolution: From obtaining food in nature to producing it. About twelve thousand years ago, some communities in Southwest Asia and elsewhere began a gradual transition from gathering and hunting to cultivation and animal husbandry. The Agricultural Revolution had begun. Parts of China, New Guinea, Africa, and the Americas subsequently developed their own agriculture. Domestication and improvements in cultivation and storage allowed people to organize food production more actively. Agriculture gradually increased the population that land could support, laying material foundations for population growth, settlement, and specialization. This was the revolution’s most far-reaching change.

Changes in subsistence reshaped society. Settlements grew, and more people could work in crafts, exchange, and administration. Communities in different environments developed farming, pastoralism, and other ways of making a living, which coexisted with gathering and hunting over long periods. Agricultural knowledge initially spread chiefly through migration and oral transmission, often over millennia. The timing of these changes consequently varied widely across the world. This revolution gradually shifted the basis of subsistence toward plants and animals that people cultivated and managed themselves.

The Communication Revolution: Carrying information beyond personal memory and face-to-face contact. A new wave of invention beginning more than five thousand years ago is often described through the Bronze and Iron Ages, named for the many metal objects recovered by archaeologists. From this book’s perspective, writing, numbers, and record-keeping deserve particular emphasis. People began to store information outside the human body, allowing knowledge to travel across distances and generations. Accounts, calendars, laws, and stories entered enduring information networks, providing new foundations for complex administration and cultural transmission.

The formative core of this Communication Revolution can be divided into two phases. From the late fourth millennium BCE to around the eighth century BCE, several civilizations developed writing, counting, arithmetic, measurement, and associated recording tools. Basic means of creating, preserving, and retrieving information took shape, supporting cities, trade, and kingdoms. The second phase centers on the Axial Age, from the eighth century BCE through the centuries around the beginning of the Common Era. Philosophy, religion, and political thought flourished, while mathematics, astronomy, and other bodies of knowledge became more systematic. Writing materials, textual media, and education allowed these ideas to be studied, interpreted, and transmitted repeatedly, forming enduring cores of several major civilizations.

Transport and trade widened the transformation. Horses, camels, vehicles, and ships carried people and knowledge farther. Eurasian overland trade routes and sea lanes connected the accumulated knowledge of several civilizations. Paper, printing, gunpowder, and other technologies passed through successive communities and acquired new uses in new societies. Oceanic voyages from the fifteenth century onward further connected the continents, accelerating flows of knowledge, goods, and people. The Communication Revolution left humanity an expandable capability: gathering scattered experience into wider knowledge networks from which later generations could create anew.

The Efficiency Revolution: Using new energy sources, machines, and scientific knowledge to exceed the limits of muscle power and traditional production. From the seventeenth century onward, Europe’s Scientific and Industrial Revolutions opened a new phase. Coal and other fossil fuels gradually expanded the sources of power. Steam engines, internal combustion engines, generators, and electric motors enabled continuous production on a larger scale. Textile machinery, precision manufacturing, and new materials increased processing speed, accuracy, and the effective use of resources. Thermodynamics, electromagnetism, and other sciences interacted with invention, turning efficiency improvement into a continuing field of investigation and development.

The most striking social outcome was a substantial increase in productive capacity and the wealth available per person. Machines enabled the same amount of labor to produce more, and energy conversion made power far beyond human muscle available for use. Many once-expensive goods entered ordinary households. Medicine, transport, communication, and research gained stronger material foundations. The author brings the modern Scientific and Industrial Revolutions together under the Efficiency Revolution to emphasize how breakthroughs in different fields jointly transformed humanity’s capacity to create wealth and fulfill aspirations.

The Efficiency Revolution continues. Energy transitions, new materials, and intelligent machines keep changing production and work, while modern communication and computing connect these capabilities into larger systems of cooperation. Sustainable energy may further expand the limits of resource use. Artificial intelligence extends efficiency gains into knowledge creation, design, and execution. Energy constraints, environmental costs, and the distribution of benefits remain problems to resolve as the revolution advances—and continuing sources of demand for innovation.

The three revolutions changed the basic ways humans obtain food, accumulate knowledge, and create wealth. The Subsistence Revolution supported larger populations and greater specialization. The Communication Revolution enabled knowledge to accumulate across places and generations. The Efficiency Revolution converted that accumulation into expanding productive capabilities and possibilities for life. Along these three lines, scattered inventions and discoveries reveal shared historical directions: humanity progressively enlarged its capacity to sustain life, connect people, and fulfill aspirations.

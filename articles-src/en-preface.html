Preface to the English Edition

Why do humans innovate? How does innovation change life? And why does it take different forms in different places and periods?

Jielin Dong

This first English edition is based on the second Chinese edition of The Power of Desire: A Brief History of Human Technological Innovation. The book first appeared in Chinese in 2019. Since then, artificial intelligence has moved beyond a handful of specialist fields into everyday life, participating in writing, programming, design, and research. Energy transitions, electric vehicles, and robotics have become arenas of intense industrial competition. Population aging and changing international relations have brought new challenges and opportunities for innovation. These developments prompted a substantial revision of the Chinese book. The English edition presents that expanded account to a wider readership.

The book is concerned with three questions: Why do humans innovate? How does innovation change life? And why does it take different forms in different places and periods? Taking human desires and needs as its thread, the original Chinese edition brought discoveries and inventions scattered across disciplines into a shared historical picture. The second Chinese edition developed that approach through a clearer account of the method, richer historical narratives, and discussion of important recent developments. This English edition draws on that work to offer a history of innovation that helps us understand the past while providing clues for judging the future.

In the preface to the original Chinese edition, the author expressed the hope that historical stories and research would pay tribute to the innovators who have shaped science and technology. Many left no names behind; others paid a price for questioning authority or departing from convention. The knowledge and objects we now take for granted embody their intelligence, persistence, and willingness to take risks. That purpose continues here: to attend to these creators and to how societies can make room for their explorations.

Two narrative approaches support one another throughout the book. Historical narrative follows people, inventions, and processes of diffusion; structural narrative uses data to examine long-term change and compare regional differences. The former reveals the circumstances in which creation took place, while the latter brings a larger pattern into view. Bringing the two together is the author’s attempt to understand scientific and technological innovation.

Building on the research and revisions behind the Chinese editions, this English edition brings together an analytical framework, historical accounts, statistical evidence, and an exploration of technological change. Five features of this approach deserve introduction.

First, the book explains how its classification framework was developed and how it is used. People create to obtain food, secure protection, communicate, act independently, develop their abilities, and explore the unknown. Markets, culture, and institutions shape the choices they make. How personal aspirations become shared tasks, and how social needs become the actions of particular individuals, are central to the book’s understanding of the forces behind innovation.

Maslow’s theory of needs provides an analytical scaffold for this discussion. Following this thread, the book organizes innovation into six categories: Subsistence, Security and Health, Communication and Entertainment, Mobility and Flexibility, Efficiency and Utilization, and Exploration and Transcendence. It explains the reasoning that connects a theory of needs to a classification of science and technology, as well as the relationships between the categories. A technology can serve several needs, and different desires often coexist. The classification’s value lies in revealing connections between scattered historical materials, helping us see which problems creative activity initially addressed and how those problems changed with society.

This framework brings three great revolutions into focus: the Subsistence Revolution, the Communication Revolution, and the Efficiency Revolution. Agriculture changed how people obtained food; writing, calculation, and information transmission changed how knowledge accumulated and societies cooperated; energy, machines, and modern science changed how wealth was created. Achievements that initially seem far apart thus become parts of connected historical processes. These three themes connect the parts and chapters, allowing broad observations and individual stories to illuminate one another.

Second, the historical accounts place inventions within the lives and working environments in which they emerged. To understand earlier generations’ creative efforts, the author visited museums and ancient sites around the world. An achievement often results from a succession of contributions: someone proposes a principle, another makes a tool, others improve the process, and still others take the result to new places and put it to new uses. The narrative develops the connections between these stages while preserving the creators’ circumstances, choices, and imaginative efforts, making it easier to understand why a technology emerged and how it acquired the power to change society.

The history of science and technology also contains many gaps. Archaeological evidence, written records, and later traditions can answer different kinds of questions. In preparing the revised Chinese manuscript on which this edition is based, the author checked and corrected facts that could be verified. Where a historical conjecture remains uncertain but has explanatory value, the book seeks to clarify both the grounds for the conjecture and its nature. Historical research requires connections to be drawn between surviving clues. Interesting, reasonable conjectures can also help us ask new questions. The author hopes to preserve room for such exploration while enabling readers to distinguish known facts from interpretations of them.

Third, the Database of Major Scientific and Technological Achievements has been updated through 2025, and the statistical figures have been remade accordingly. The book uses counts of major first achievements to examine historical changes at the technological frontier and compare their distribution across periods, regions, and categories of need. The statistics and discussion have been revised together. Hatching and grayscale make categories and regions easier to distinguish in black-and-white printing. The appendix further explains entry selection, dating, regional attribution, and classification by needs, and organizes the sources to help readers understand how the results were produced.

The relationship between major breakthroughs and incremental innovation is a particular concern of this edition. A few major breakthroughs open new possibilities; many continuing improvements make new capabilities reliable, affordable, and widely available. New applications, in turn, raise further scientific and technological questions. Under the book’s statistical conventions, education, the research workforce, and research funding expanded substantially over the past century, yet the annual number of major first achievements per person declined markedly. This contrast deserves close attention. How can an increasingly rich array of products and applications generate the next round of major breakthroughs? How can large research systems leave room for explorations that depart from the mainstream? These questions run through the opening, the chapters, and the conclusion.

Fourth, the book examines contemporary technological developments in their historical contexts, with particular attention to artificial intelligence (AI). Breakthroughs in large models have brought AI into knowledge production and the execution of complex tasks. Alongside a chapter devoted to AI, the book explores its effects on medicine, mathematics, transport, manufacturing, intellectual property, and other fields. In medicine, for example, protein structure prediction and open databases provide new foundations for life-science research. AI is also entering the analysis of experimental results, drug development, clinical and genetic diagnosis, personalized prescribing, and surgical assistance. These advances are bringing basic research and clinical practice into closer contact.

AI and its development toward superintelligence (SI) will affect all six categories discussed in the book, changing the entire process from posing questions and discovering principles to design, production, and application. The author believes this will initiate an industrial revolution exceeding the internet wave and even the revolution driven by the steam engine. Sharply lower execution costs enable individuals and microbusinesses with ideas to draw on capabilities once available only to larger organizations. Global manufacturing and service supply chains, divisions of labor within firms, and research organizations will also be restructured. The book therefore reexamines the relationships among innovation, employment, wealth distribution, and individual creativity.

Energy, new materials, electric vehicles, and robotics also receive attention. Chips, electricity, materials, equipment, and industrial networks are interdependent. Strength in foundational links can support higher-level applications while also providing leverage over other countries. Whether prosperity can endure after an energy transition, how intelligent machines change production and employment, and how critical materials and supply chains become instruments of national competition are central questions. Alternative energy scenarios clarify the different paths that breakthroughs and resource constraints may produce.

Fifth, the conclusion connects explanations of the past with judgments about the future through five sections: historical patterns, the factors shaping them, the intelligence revolution, demographic change, and U.S.–China competition. Accounts of regional innovation and its underlying mechanisms connect statistical observations with historical experience. The forward-looking sections begin with the new industrial revolution initiated by AI/SI, examining changes in research, enterprises, service and manufacturing networks, and the geography of energy. A separate section on U.S.–China technological competition moves from the countries’ core needs, institutions, and resource allocation to competition across the links of intelligent industries, and offers a judgment about who is more likely to lead the next technological wave.

The demographic discussion has a distinct purpose in the opening and the conclusion. The opening examines the historical correlation between population size and major innovation; the conclusion focuses on how age structure and talent mobility may reshape the technological landscape. China’s past demographic dividend and the effects of decline and aging on production, markets, and innovation are considered alongside America’s ability to attract global talent, Europe’s scientific strengths and aging pressures, and opportunities in regions with younger populations. All are examined in the context of the intelligence revolution. As machines take over more execution, the development and movement of creators, and their room to explore, become more important.

The book offers explicit forecasts. AI-assisted research may generate clusters of mutually reinforcing major first achievements in future, opening a new period of accelerating creativity and potentially reversing the decline in annual major first achievements per person. Shrinking societies may become the most automated. China and America may both declare victory, drawing on different strengths and different measures of success. The author hopes these judgments will encourage readers to imagine the future while leaving a record that time can test.

Readers can follow the book in sequence to trace connections among needs, creation, and social change, or begin with chapters that interest them. Those most concerned with long-term statistical patterns and regional comparisons may read the opening, the six part introductions, and the conclusion together, then consult the appendix on the database and classification methods. Readers more interested in people and inventions will find relatively self-contained accounts in each of the twenty-four chapters.

Research and writing for the Chinese editions benefited from the help of colleagues, students, scholars, friends, and family members. Their contributions remain part of the foundation of this English edition, and the author thanks them once again. Responsibility for the book’s factual judgments, historical interpretations, and predictions remains with the author. The hope is that readers will not only discover how earlier generations created today’s world, but also bring their own experiences and questions to thinking about the next chapter in the history of innovation.

Jielin Dong

"A Small Boat on the Long River of Human Innovation History" · Xue Lan (Professor, Tsinghua University; Dean, Schwarzman College)

A Small Boat on the Long River of Human Innovation History

Xue Lan (Professor at Tsinghua University; Dean of Schwarzman College)

To borrow a fashionable phrase: this is a book destined to catch fire!

Innovation is arguably the most fashionable concept in China today. From major government policies and international trade disputes to college-student startups and stock-market swings, everything is tied to innovation. But what drives human innovation? Why do some people leave illustrious names in the history of innovation while others remain unknown? What makes some innovations hugely successful while others end in bitter regret? What twists, turns, and surprises has humanity met on the road of innovation — and where will that road take us?

To answer these questions, we could study the classics of the history of science and technology, or comb the latest research in academic journals. But in an age when time is so highly compressed, most people shrink from that prospect. Nor are historians of science keen to spend their time writing a book that both attracts general readers and remains accurate and comprehensive — in today's world, where papers are everything, it seems a thankless task. Dr. Jielin Dong's book happens to meet this enormous social need.

Building on her systematic scholarly research into the history of innovation, the author deconstructs the traditional chronicle of scientific and technological innovation and rebuilds it within a new framework, using beautiful prose, captivating stories, and rigorous academic analysis — achieving an effect reminiscent of the recent bestseller Sapiens. The resulting masterpiece is like a small boat on the long river of human innovation history, letting us drift at leisure through its splendid chapters, savoring how humanity's many needs have been met and elevated in the course of innovation, and filling us with fresh expectations for its future. Combining a rigorous analytical framework, wonderful historical stories, a distinctive perspective and viewpoint, and exquisite photographs and illustrations, this book is an irresistible treasure trove of innovation that readers simply cannot put down.

Dr. Jielin Dong was, so to speak, my senior schoolmate in the United States. Early on, through the famous CUSPEA program (the China-U.S. Physics Examination and Application Program), she went to the Department of Physics at Carnegie Mellon University to pursue her PhD. After graduating in 1988, she worked in scientific research and also distinguished herself in the financial markets. In recent years she returned to China and devoted herself to research on innovation. At Soochow University she established an innovation research center, leading a group of young scholars and students in collecting large quantities of data with the rigorous methods and attitude of the natural sciences, building a database of human achievements in scientific and technological innovation, producing a great deal of interesting research, and publishing many important academic papers. She also graciously accepted our invitation to hold a concurrent appointment at Tsinghua University's Research Center for Technological Innovation. The publication of this book is both expected and unexpected. Expected, because consolidating important earlier research into a book is what many scholars do. Unexpected, because what she has published is not a typical academic monograph but this delightful "unofficial history" of innovation. In the book's preface, the author herself gives the answer: since childhood, she has cherished both the "white rose" dream of becoming a scientist and the "red rose" dream of becoming a writer. With this history of human innovation, her "red rose" and "white rose" dreams have come true at once.

The main body of the book tells the stories of human innovation. Building on Maslow's hierarchy of needs, the author proposes a "hierarchy of human needs" framework for classifying science and technology, dividing technological achievements into six categories: survival/sustenance, safety/health, communication/entertainment, mobility/flexibility, efficiency/utilization, and exploration/transcendence. Along these six threads, the narrative proceeds in two ways. First, with a wealth of fascinating stories and exquisite photographs and images, the author shows us the rich, colorful history of innovation in human development, vividly re-creating the figures, great and small, who contributed to it. Within each thread, she gives particular description and analysis to China's innovative contributions in that field. Her beautiful, fluent prose and rigorous scholarly verification not only breathe new life into these historical facts but also correct a number of false legends in circulation. Second, adopting a structural narrative approach and drawing on the database built up over years of in-depth research, she examines the structure and developmental threads of ten thousand years of science and technology history along the six lines above, compares regions horizontally, and uncovers statistical patterns within them, before synthesizing the stories of the preceding chapters. This arrangement both provides a wealth of vivid historical material on innovation — meeting the public's need to understand its history — and presents rigorous data analysis, leaving experts in the history of science and technology room to explore its many mysteries.

Another commendable quality of this book is that, on controversial questions in the history of science and technology, it does not shy away; it points the questions out and offers the author's own clear views. For example, Cai Lun's papermaking is one of the Four Great Inventions in which Chinese people take pride, yet the historical facts behind it are disputed. The author introduces one view: as early as the Western Han dynasty there was Fangmatan paper from Gansu, and later Bajiao paper from Xi'an, more than 200 years before Cai Lun. On this view, Cai Lun was not the original inventor of papermaking but an improver of the papermaking process. Another view holds that Western Han paper was at best a prototype of paper, incomparable in quality to "Marquis Cai's paper." This book sides with the Western Han view. Whichever position one takes, the case of Cai Lun shows us that a major innovation is rarely achieved in a single leap: there may be flashes of inspiration, but more often it is gradual, incremental innovation.

Another celebrated case in the history of science involves Raymond Damadian, inventor of magnetic resonance imaging, the American scientist Paul Lauterbur, and the British scientist Peter Mansfield. This case once compelled the U.S. National Academy of Sciences to organize a special team to study and analyze the three men's contributions. Damadian was among the earliest scientists to apply nuclear magnetic resonance to cancer research; he discovered that the NMR relaxation times of tumor tissue and normal tissue differ, and proposed using this difference to detect tumors. Yet the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Lauterbur and Mansfield for their subsequent contributions in this area. While expressing sympathy for Damadian, the author also subtly conveys her own view: scientific evaluation should be based on facts, and should not be swayed by a scientist's personality or character.

The author also clears up several common misunderstandings in society about innovation. For example, when Tu Youyou won the Nobel Prize, Chinese social media expressed regret: the Chinese had invented artemisinin, yet suffered great economic losses for failing to apply for a patent. But the artemisinin research involved discovering that the artemisinin in the Artemisia plant has antimalarial effects — that part is a scientific discovery, and scientific discoveries themselves cannot be patented. How to extract the active ingredient artemisinin, and how to turn it into a drug suitable for human use, are technical questions, which can be patented when they meet the conditions for patent grants. After the active ingredient was published as a scientific result, many manufacturers could develop different artemisinin extraction technologies and manufacture drugs; the commercial value of an extraction technology patent also depends on factors such as the scope of protection and alternative processes.

Surveying the whole book, the author uses vivid prose to present us with a magnificent history of human innovation; closing the volume, one cannot help but sigh with emotion. The implements and tools we take for granted in our lives today — behind them lie how much crystallized human wisdom, and how many thinkers, scientists, and skilled craftsmen contributed their talents to painting this splendid panorama of human innovation and invention! As a Chinese reader, it is hard at this moment not to think of the Needham Question: why did modern science and the Industrial Revolution not occur in China? Why have the Chinese not made more contributions to the honor roll of human innovation and invention? In Chapter 24 and the conclusion, the author analyzes and discusses these questions in depth, reaching a number of insightful conclusions that provoke deep thought. We hope that in its future development of innovation, China can learn from history, build and improve its national innovation system, remove institutional obstacles to innovation, and with firmer confidence answer the questions the author poses at the end: Can China become a trendsetter at the frontiers of human science and technology, and hold that frontier steadily and for the long term? Will it bring new solutions to human society? And what world changes will those new solutions trigger? We shall wait and see!

"A Precious Minority Voice in the Genealogy of Ideas" · Yan Yan (Founding Managing Partner, SAIF Partners)

A Precious Minority Voice in the Genealogy of Ideas

Yan Yan (Founding Managing Partner, SAIF Partners)

I first met Jielin Dong in the late 1980s and early 1990s, when we were both studying in the United States. My impression of her was of a warm, spirited, and tenacious young woman. Later I left the academic path for business, joining a fund under AIG (American International Group) as an investor, and frequently traveled back to China. I heard that she, too, was starting businesses and often shuttling between China and the U.S. But we never ran into each other again — until 2011, when I met her at a "Thousand Talents Plan" conference and she told me she was now teaching at a university and doing research. I was quite surprised, for returning to the austere life of research after making money in business is uncommon in today's China.

Not long ago she messaged me on WeChat, asking me to write a foreword for her new book on the history of human scientific and technological innovation. I agreed without hesitation — first, because I believe the subject is meaningful for China today; second, out of curiosity: I wanted to see what this extraordinary woman would write. She then sent me the manuscript in installments. To my surprise, she had spent five years writing a work of some four to five hundred thousand characters.

In this book, Dong Jielin explores questions people often ponder — for instance: Why do humans engage in scientific and technological innovation, and how do they go about it? What is the most significant technological innovation of our time, and how will it change our future lives? On the relationship between technological innovation and geography, culture, institutions, economy, and history, there are many excellent works on the market. Max Weber's classic The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, for example, explores from the perspective of religious values the relationship between the Protestant ethic and the formation of the modern capitalist spirit. What distinguishes Dong Jielin's book from others is that, building on theories established by predecessors in different fields, she erects a new analytical framework and, combining historical and contemporary big data, focuses on the relationship between technological innovation and human desires and social needs themselves.

On these questions, the book offers many interesting discussions and insights. Dong Jielin argues that human history has seen three long waves of technological revolution: the "survival technology revolution" beginning more than 10,000 years ago, corresponding to the Agricultural Revolution and the Neolithic Revolution, which laid a new foundation for population growth and triggered the great divergence between farming and nomadic peoples; the "communication technology revolution" beginning 5,000 years ago (revolutions in writing, mathematics, information, entertainment, and other fields), corresponding to a leap forward in the production and dissemination of knowledge, which drove structural changes in society, improved management efficiency, and from then on divided civilized society from barbarism; and the "efficiency technology revolution" beginning 400 years ago (including the Scientific Revolution and the Industrial Revolution) — in particular the use of fossil energy and the invention of powered machinery — which brought about enormous growth in wealth, enabled modern society to break free from traditional society, and set the modernization processes of countries around the world slowly in motion.

On the historical development of science and technology in China and Europe, she offers profound analysis and comparison using the dual method of presenting data and telling stories. She argues that geography is merely the backdrop; the divergence between Chinese and European development trajectories in science and technology over the past 2,000 years — especially their parting of ways in recent centuries — was caused mainly by three human factors: the preference for and capacity to pursue precision (as in mathematics, logic, and the like); the pluralism of thought and power; and the structure and dynamics of the commercial system. These three factors remain crucial to a society's capacity for technological innovation today and in the future.

On the future development of science and technology, Dong Jielin offers some rather unconventional judgments. Over the past century, research investment and the number of researchers have increased enormously; yet by her database's statistics, per-capita annual major scientific and technological firsts peaked around the 1920s and have declined markedly since. That products are updated faster and faster does not necessarily mean that major breakthroughs are becoming more numerous. Her analysis of China's history of science and technology also departs from the usual narrative: traditional Chinese technology did lead in a number of fields, but counting science and technology together and measuring by the cumulative number of major firsts, Europe has long had more. Looking ahead, she believes that AI and its development toward superintelligence (SI) may reverse the declining trend in per-capita major firsts and open a new industrial revolution; yet growth in output and wealth may not bring synchronized growth in most people's wages — the price of much execution-type work may instead decline. Some of these judgments come from statistics, others are bold extrapolations about the future; all deserve serious discussion. If opinions in the marketplace of ideas are especially uniform, that is not necessarily a good thing. In the genealogy of ideas, those well-grounded minority voices that dare to offer different explanations are especially precious.

"Why Do Humans Innovate?" · Sun Xiaochun (Professor, School of Humanities, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; former President, Chinese Society for the History of Science and Technology)

Why Do Humans Innovate? — Foreword to the Second Edition of The Power of Desire: A Brief History of Scientific & Technological Innovation

Sun Xiaochun (Professor, School of Humanities, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; former President, Chinese Society for the History of Science and Technology)

Since the publication of the first edition of The Power of Desire: A Brief History of Scientific & Technological Innovation, the world's science and technology have undergone dazzling change. Artificial intelligence has rapidly entered social life; the life sciences, clean energy, space exploration, and digital technology continually expand the boundaries of human capability. At the same time, the risks, ethical dilemmas, and social divisions brought about by technological innovation attract more attention than ever before. Against this backdrop, this book is about to appear in the United States in a second edition, published separately in Chinese and English — most timely indeed. It prompts us to confront once again a seemingly simple yet fundamental question: Why do humans innovate?

My acquaintance with Professor Jielin Dong began with academic exchanges on the history of science and technology and questions of innovation. Professor Dong graduated from the Department of Physics at Sun Yat-sen University and, in the 1980s, went to Carnegie Mellon University in the United States for her doctorate through CUSPEA (the China-U.S. Physics Examination and Application Program). She later took up writing, serving as a contributing writer for The Wall Street Journal in the United States and the Financial Times in Britain, and has served as a Distinguished Professor at the School of Business of Soochow University and an adjunct professor at Tsinghua University's Research Center for Technological Innovation. With training in the natural sciences, experience in business and social observation, and a background in writing — as well as her use of scientometric methods to compile a chronology of world scientific and technological invention — she brings a distinctive perspective to observing the history of science and technology.

On behalf of the Chinese Society for the History of Science and Technology, I once invited her to Beijing to give an academic lecture entitled "Looking Back over Ten Thousand Years: How Has Technological Development Met Human Needs?" She attempted to explain humanity's scientific and technological inventions using Maslow's theory of needs. Not all participants fully agreed with all of her judgments, but her quantitative work on the world's scientific and technological inventions and creations, and her effort to reconstruct the history of innovation starting from human needs, earned everyone's affirmation. Science and technology are among the most cumulative and progressive of human civilization's enterprises, and their long and complex history can indeed gain a new presentation with the aid of data and quantitative methods.

In the years of academic exchange since, I have seen Professor Dong's horizons continually widen. While holding fast to the value of scientific progress, she has paid ever greater attention to the social and cultural dimensions of science, reading earnestly in anthropology, religious studies, and the philosophy of science, and reflecting on the relationships among primitive thought, magic, religion, science, and different knowledge traditions. She has also visited museums around the world, conducted field investigations of ancient sites, and built a "Database of Major Human Scientific and Technological Achievements." The Power of Desire: A Brief History of Scientific & Technological Innovation is the crystallization of this long accumulation, extensive reading, and firsthand observation.

Why do humans innovate? Why invent new technologies? Professor Jielin Dong answers these questions by examining the history of human innovation. Over humanity's journey from the distant past, what traits set humans apart from other animals? Humans are social animals who must form societies and establish governance orders — hence "political man" (Homo politicus). Human sexual behavior is not confined to a particular stage of the reproductive cycle — hence "sexual man" (Homo sexualis). Humans think — Homo sapiens. And humans also make tools and create new things — "maker man" (Homo faber). To know and transform the world by means of tools is among the most defining traits of humanity. Technology is not something attached to human life from the outside; it is a basic fact of human life itself. Without technological innovation, there would be none of the human civilization we know.

To ponder questions of such grandeur unquestionably takes courage. Professor Dong skillfully applies Maslow's theory of needs, treating needs and desires as important drivers of human innovation — a main thread running through the whole book. Maslow ranked human needs from low to high: physiological needs, safety needs, social needs, esteem needs, and self-actualization needs, later also discussing the need for self-transcendence. Within this framework, technological innovation can be understood as humanity's continuing effort to satisfy one or more of these needs.

The main body of the book unfolds largely along the hierarchy of needs, stringing the history of technological innovation since ancient times along the clear thread of "needs." Frankly, assigning each technological innovation to a particular level of need inevitably admits of differing views. Yet the classification in this book is no arbitrary patchwork; it embodies the author's long reflection on the relationship between technological innovation and human society. The effort she has invested is evident from the "Database of Major Human Scientific and Technological Achievements" that she built for this book alone. She writes not only with materials and data, but also with thought and feeling.

Reading this manuscript was a pleasurable experience, and often a surprising one. Professor Dong is adept at observing and experiencing the history of human invention and creation from the perspective of the technology's user, which gives her grand narrative of the history of technology a vivid sense of lived life. In the chapter "The Innovative World of the Foodie," for example, she strings together innovations spanning ten thousand years and belonging to different fields — agricultural inventions, ceramics, food processing, culinary arts, wine, soy sauce, cheese, and vitamin C — presenting a historical panorama of "innovation by everyone." In the chapter "The Cheapness Revolution and the Secret of GDP Growth," she discusses how new products stimulate and even create material needs, and how standardized production lowers costs and prices, bringing products into broader social life and thereby driving the growth of wealth. Along this line of thought, innovations in product mix, production management, the spread of electricity, standardization, and automation are linked together to form the technological picture of the Industrial Revolution.

In such a narrative, technological inventions become the material elements for understanding the history of human civilization, and the history of technology unfolds along multiple dimensions at once — scientific, social, cultural, and psychological. Readers no longer see merely the internal details of some invention, but its position within the network of human needs and its connections with institutions, ideas, and ways of life. This is the book's most illuminating quality: it does not write innovation merely as a heroic history of a few geniuses' flashes of inspiration, but restores it as a historical process in which countless individuals and groups respond to survival needs, social aspirations, and spiritual pursuits.

Another valuable quality of this book is the author's willingness, in the course of discussion, continually to revise her own understanding. On questions such as the relationship among magic, religion, and science; whether ancient China had science; and why modern science did not arise in China, academia has always held differing opinions. Professor Dong listens carefully to different views, reads the relevant literature, and forms her own judgments. She no longer treats magic simply as the opposite of science or as "superstition," but understands it as the practice by which early humans, within their particular worldview, mode of thinking, and value system, sought to understand and cope with the world. The book's absorption of the research of anthropologists such as Frazer, Malinowski, and Evans-Pritchard demonstrates an author adept at self-criticism and self-revision, continually expanding the boundaries of her thought.

The publication of the second edition in the United States, in Chinese and English, carries a further significance. Technological innovation has never been an isolated process within a single civilization; it unfolds in the interaction, competition, borrowing, and transformation among different societies. To narrate the global history of technological innovation along the thread of human needs can help readers of different cultural backgrounds cross the familiar boundaries of region and discipline, see what is common in human experience, and see the differences in how civilizations choose, use, and evaluate technology. At a time when technological development is increasingly globalized while the world faces the risk of fragmentation, this kind of cross-cultural writing on the history of technology is particularly worthy of attention.

This book covers a great many technological innovations and inventions, touching nearly every aspect of human needs. No author could render a final verdict on every invention in so long a history, nor is that this book's purpose. It is more like a grand thought experiment on a vast scale: taking needs theory as the warp and technological innovation as the weft, it attempts to construct an overall picture of the evolution of human civilization. History has no ready-made trajectory; it is researchers who, through materials, questions, and theories, endow history with an intelligible structure — and such constructions will surely continue amid new materials, new questions, and new experiences of the age. The publication of the second edition is a new starting point on this intellectual journey. I extend my congratulations to Professor Jielin Dong, and am glad once again to recommend this work — full of courage, insight, and creativity — to Chinese and English readers alike.

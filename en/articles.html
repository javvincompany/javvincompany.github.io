"A Small Boat on the Great River of Human Innovation" · Xue Lan (Professor, Tsinghua University; Dean, Schwarzman College)

A Small Boat on the Great River of Human Innovation

Xue Lan (Professor at Tsinghua University and Dean of Schwarzman College, Tsinghua University)

To borrow a popular expression, this is a book destined to be a hit!

Innovation must be one of the most fashionable concepts in China today. From government policy to international trade disputes, from student entrepreneurship to stock-market gains and losses, it is everywhere. But what drives human innovation? Why do some people become celebrated figures in its history while others remain anonymous? What makes some innovations spectacularly successful while others end in disappointing failure? What setbacks and surprises have people encountered along the way? And where will the future of innovation take us?

To answer these questions, we could study classic works on the history of science and technology or consult the latest research in academic journals. In an age when time is so tightly squeezed, however, most people find that prospect daunting. Specialists in the field, meanwhile, are often reluctant to devote time to writing a book that appeals to a broad readership while remaining accurate and comprehensive. In today’s publication-driven academic world, that can seem a thankless undertaking. Dr. Jielin Dong’s book meets precisely this substantial public need.

Drawing on systematic scholarly research into the history of innovation, the author dismantles the conventional chronology of scientific and technological achievements and reconstructs it within a new framework, using elegant prose, compelling stories, and rigorous analysis. In this respect, the book has something in common with the widely popular Sapiens. The resulting work is like a small boat on the great river of human innovation, carrying us effortlessly through its remarkable episodes. We see how human needs have been met and transformed through innovation, and find fresh reasons to look forward to what comes next. Combining a rigorous analytical framework, vivid historical stories, distinctive perspectives, and beautiful photography and illustrations, this is a treasury of innovation that is hard to put down.

Dr. Dong preceded me among Chinese students pursuing graduate study in the United States. She went to Carnegie Mellon University’s Department of Physics through the well-known China–United States Physics Examination and Application program (CUSPEA). After receiving her doctorate in 1988, she worked in scientific research and later ventured into the financial markets. Some years ago, she returned to China and devoted herself to research on innovation. At Soochow University, she established an innovation research center and led a group of young scholars and students in gathering extensive data with the rigor and discipline of the natural sciences. They built a database of human scientific and technological achievements, conducted many interesting studies, and published important research papers. She also readily accepted our invitation to take up a part-time appointment at Tsinghua University’s Research Center for Science and Technology Policy. The publication of this book is both understandable and unexpected. It is understandable because scholars commonly bring their earlier research together in book form. It is unexpected because she has produced not a conventional academic monograph but this delightful, more personal history of innovation. The author explains why in her preface: since childhood, she has cherished both the “white rose” dream of becoming a scientist and the “red rose” dream of becoming a writer. This history of human innovation has finally allowed both dreams to blossom together.

At its heart, the book tells the stories of human innovation. Building on Maslow’s hierarchy of human needs, the author proposes a needs-based classification of science and technology with six broad categories: Subsistence, Security and Health, Communication and Entertainment, Mobility and Flexibility, Efficiency and Utilization, and Exploration and Transcendence. Along these six threads, the narrative develops in two complementary ways. First, engaging stories and beautiful photographs and illustrations reveal the rich variety of innovation throughout human development, bringing to life both famous and lesser-known contributors. Within each thread, the author pays particular attention to China’s contributions. Her fluent prose and careful investigation breathe new life into these historical accounts while also correcting a number of popular misconceptions. Second, she uses structural narrative, drawing on the database built through years of research to examine the organization and development of ten thousand years of science and technology along the same six threads. She compares regions, identifies statistical patterns, and brings together the stories told in the preceding chapters. This arrangement offers abundant, vivid historical material for general readers while providing rigorous analysis through which specialists can explore unresolved questions in the history of innovation.

Another valuable feature is the author’s willingness to confront controversies in the history of science and technology and state her own views clearly. Cai Lun’s papermaking, for example, is celebrated as one of China’s Four Great Inventions, yet the historical details remain disputed. The author presents the view that paper from Fangmatan in Gansu, dating to the Western Han dynasty, and the later paper from Baqiao in Xi’an predate Cai Lun’s work by more than two hundred years. From this perspective, Cai Lun improved the papermaking process rather than inventing it. Another view holds that Western Han paper was at most an early precursor, far inferior in quality to the paper associated with Cai Lun. This book accepts the case for papermaking in the Western Han period. Whatever position one takes, the example shows how rarely a major innovation is achieved in a single stroke. A flash of inspiration may play a part, but much of the work proceeds through incremental improvements.

Another controversy concerns Raymond Damadian, Paul Lauterbur of the United States, and Peter Mansfield of Britain, and their contributions to magnetic resonance imaging. The dispute prompted the U.S. National Academy of Sciences to assemble a team to examine their respective contributions. Damadian was among the early scientists to apply nuclear magnetic resonance to tumor research. He found differences in relaxation times between tumor tissue and normal tissue and proposed using those differences to detect tumors. Yet the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to Lauterbur and Mansfield, who made subsequent contributions in this field. In expressing sympathy for Damadian, the author also implicitly makes her position clear: scientific contributions should be evaluated on the evidence, without being swayed by a scientist’s character or personality.

The author also clarifies several common misunderstandings about innovation. When Tu Youyou received the Nobel Prize, for example, commentators on Chinese social media lamented that China had suffered considerable economic losses because artemisinin had not been patented. Research on artemisinin, however, involved discovering the antimalarial properties of the compound found in sweet wormwood. That is a scientific discovery, which in itself cannot be patented. Methods for extracting the active compound and turning it into a medicine suitable for human use are technological matters and may be patented if they meet the requirements for patent protection. Once the findings identifying artemisinin as the active ingredient had been published, many manufacturers could develop different extraction methods and produce medicines. The commercial value of a patent on an extraction method also depends on the scope of its protection and the availability of alternative processes.

Taken as a whole, the book vividly presents an extraordinary history of human innovation, leaving much to reflect on after the final page. How much human ingenuity lies behind the everyday objects and tools we now take for granted! How many thinkers, scientists, and skilled craftspeople contributed to this magnificent panorama of invention! For a Chinese reader, it is difficult not to return to the Needham Question: Why did modern science and the Industrial Revolution not emerge in China? Why have Chinese people not contributed more to the roll of honor of human invention? In Chapter 24 and the conclusion, the author examines these questions and reaches insightful, thought-provoking conclusions. We hope China will learn from history as it pursues innovation, build and improve its national innovation system, remove institutional obstacles, and gain the confidence to address the author’s final questions: Can China become a leading force at the scientific and technological frontier and hold that position over the long term? Will it offer new solutions to human society? What changes might those solutions bring to the world? We await the answers with anticipation.

It is a great pleasure to learn that the second edition of A Brief History of Human Technological Innovation is about to appear. Alongside extensive revisions and additions, this edition gives particular attention to several major questions about the development of innovation: the relationship between major breakthroughs and incremental innovation, artificial intelligence’s effects on innovation, and the comparison of different innovation models. These questions may appear separate, but they share an underlying thread: How does innovation arise, and how is it sustained?

The relationship between major breakthroughs and incremental innovation concerns the rhythm and structure of technological progress. The author has observed that incremental innovations, though small, can accumulate like rain that “nourishes all things without a sound.” Major breakthroughs, once they emerge, can have exceptionally long lives and “remain in the human world.” Without the nourishment of incremental innovation, major breakthroughs struggle to realize their full social benefits; without successive major breakthroughs, the economy will eventually slip into stagnation as returns diminish. Identifying incremental innovations with the potential to become major breakthroughs is no easy task. This makes us particularly curious about the author’s fresh insights into the question.

Artificial intelligence takes this question into a new dimension. The author endorses the view that AI and its development toward superintelligence (SI) will launch a scientific and technological revolution whose effects may exceed those of the previous wave of information technology, potentially transforming the fabric of society. Its influence will extend throughout the processes of knowledge production and technological innovation. Which parts of our understanding of innovation need to be revised, and which should be retained? The author’s reflections on these questions are another reason to look forward to this edition.

The strengths and weaknesses of different innovation models, meanwhile, run through the book’s historical narrative. The revised edition pays particular attention to the factors behind U.S.–China technological competition, including competitive strategies, institutional arrangements, education and research, and manufacturing capabilities. It seeks to provide a window onto the future of global technological competition and help readers assess it more rationally and objectively.

At a time when people are exclaiming that artificial intelligence may be humanity’s final innovation, this revised edition should help turn our attention from anxiety about innovation’s end toward a deeper consideration of its nature in the AI age and its long-term significance for human society.

Xue Lan

"A Minority Voice Worth Cherishing in the Landscape of Ideas" · Yan Yan (Founding Managing Partner, SAIF Partners)

A Minority Voice Worth Cherishing in the Landscape of Ideas

Yan Yan (Founding Managing Partner of SAIF Partners)

I first met Jielin Dong in the late 1980s or early 1990s, when we were both studying in the United States. She struck me as a warm, spirited young woman with a tenacious approach to everything she did. Later, I left scholarly pursuits for business and began investing at a fund affiliated with American International Group (AIG), which took me back to China frequently. I heard that she, too, had become an entrepreneur and was often traveling between China and the United States. Yet our paths did not cross again until 2011, when I saw her at a meeting of the Thousand Talents Program. She told me she was now teaching and conducting research at a university. I was rather surprised: in China today, it is unusual for someone who has made money as an entrepreneur to return to the materially modest life of a researcher.

Not long ago, she sent me a message on WeChat asking whether I would write a foreword to her new book on the history of human scientific and technological innovation. I agreed without hesitation, partly because I thought the subject mattered to contemporary China, and partly out of curiosity: I wanted to see what this remarkable woman would write. She then began sending me the manuscript in installments. To my surprise, she had spent five years writing a work of some four to five hundred thousand Chinese characters.

In this book, Dong explores questions that many of us have considered: Why do people innovate in science and technology, and how do they do it? What are the most important innovations of our time? How will they change our future lives? Many excellent books examine the relationships between innovation and geography, culture, institutions, economics, and history. Max Weber’s celebrated The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, for example, examines the relationship between Protestant ethics and the emergence of the spirit of modern capitalism through the lens of religious values. What distinguishes Dong’s book is that she builds a new analytical framework on theories developed by earlier scholars in different fields. Combining extensive historical and contemporary datasets, she focuses on the relationship between scientific and technological innovation and people’s own desires and social needs.

The book offers many engaging discussions and insights into these questions. Dong identifies three long waves of scientific and technological revolution in human history. The Subsistence Revolution began more than ten thousand years ago, encompassing the Agricultural and Neolithic Revolutions. It established a new foundation for population growth and set farming and pastoral peoples on diverging paths. The Communication Revolution began five thousand years ago, with advances in writing, mathematics, information, and entertainment. It brought a leap in the production and transmission of knowledge, transformed social structures, and improved the efficiency of administration, marking the divergence between what have traditionally been called civilized and barbarian societies. The Efficiency Revolution began four hundred years ago and includes the Scientific and Industrial Revolutions. The use of fossil fuels and the invention of power machinery, in particular, generated enormous increases in wealth. Modern society emerged from traditional society, and modernization gradually spread across the world.

Using both data and stories, Dong offers a penetrating analysis and comparison of the historical development of science and technology in China and Europe. She sees geography as the backdrop. Over the past two thousand years, the differences between their trajectories—and especially their divergence over the past few centuries—have arisen chiefly from three human factors: the preference and capacity for precision, as expressed in mathematics and logic; pluralism in ideas and political power; and the structure and dynamics of commercial systems. These three factors are also crucial to a society’s capacity for innovation today and in the future.

Dong makes some strikingly unconventional judgments about the future of science and technology. Over the past century, research investment and the number of researchers have increased dramatically. Yet according to the book’s database, the annual number of major scientific and technological firsts per person peaked around the 1920s and then declined markedly. Faster product turnover does not necessarily mean more major breakthroughs. Her analysis of Chinese scientific and technological history also departs from familiar accounts. Traditional Chinese technologies once led in several fields, but when science and technology are counted together and measured by the cumulative number of major firsts, Europe held the larger total over long periods. Looking ahead, she believes that AI and its development toward superintelligence (SI) may reverse the decline in major firsts per person and usher in a new industrial revolution. Yet rising output and wealth may not bring corresponding wage increases for most people; the price of many kinds of work that involve carrying out tasks may actually fall. Some of these judgments arise from statistics, while others are bold projections. All deserve serious discussion. A marketplace of views in which opinions line up too neatly is not necessarily a healthy one. Minority voices that offer grounded arguments and dare to propose different interpretations are especially worth cherishing in the landscape of ideas.

"Why Do Humans Innovate" · Sun Xiaochun (Professor, School of Humanities, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; former President, Chinese Society for the History of Science and Technology)

Why Do Humans Innovate

Sun Xiaochun (Professor, School of Humanities, University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; Former President of the Chinese Society for the History of Science and Technology)

Since the first Chinese edition of this book appeared, science and technology have undergone a dizzying succession of changes. Artificial intelligence has rapidly entered social life, while the life sciences, clean energy, space exploration, and digital technologies continue to extend the boundaries of human capability. At the same time, the risks, ethical dilemmas, and social divisions arising from technological innovation have drawn greater attention than ever. Against this background, the forthcoming publication in the United States of the second Chinese edition and the first English edition, based on that revised Chinese edition, is particularly timely. It invites us to return to a seemingly simple yet fundamental question: Why do humans innovate?

I came to know Professor Jielin Dong through academic exchanges on the history of science and technology and on innovation. After graduating from the Department of Physics at Sun Yat-sen University, she went to Carnegie Mellon University in the United States in the 1980s to pursue a doctorate through the China–United States Physics Examination and Application program (CUSPEA). She later became a writer, contributing to The Wall Street Journal and the Financial Times. She also served as a distinguished professor at Soochow University’s business school and as an adjunct professor at Tsinghua University’s Research Center for Technological Innovation. Alongside her training in the natural sciences, she brings experience in business, social observation, and writing. She has also used scientometric methods to compile a chronology of scientific and technological discoveries and inventions from around the world. This academic and professional background gives her a distinctive perspective on the history of science and technology.

On behalf of the Chinese Society for the History of Science and Technology, I once invited her to Beijing to give a lecture entitled “Looking Back over Ten Thousand Years: How Science and Technology Have Met Human Needs.” She sought to explain scientific and technological invention through Maslow’s theory of needs. Those present did not necessarily agree with all her judgments, but they appreciated both her quantitative work on inventions worldwide and her effort to reconstruct the history of innovation around human needs. Science and technology are among the most cumulative and progressive of human endeavors. Data and quantitative methods can indeed reveal their long and complex history in new ways.

Over the years of our subsequent academic exchanges, I have watched Professor Dong’s outlook broaden. While continuing to affirm the value of scientific progress, she has become increasingly attentive to the social and cultural dimensions of science. She has read extensively in anthropology, religious studies, and the philosophy of science, reflecting on the relationships among early forms of thought, magic, religion, science, and different traditions of knowledge. She has also visited museums and ancient sites around the world and established the Database of Major Scientific and Technological Achievements. This book is the fruit of those years of accumulated research, wide reading, and firsthand observation.

Why do humans innovate? Why do they invent new technologies? Professor Dong approaches these questions through the history of human innovation. As humans emerged from the distant past, what characteristics distinguished them from other animals? Humans live in groups, form societies, and establish systems of governance: we are political beings, Homo politicus. Human sexual activity is not confined to a particular phase of the reproductive cycle: we are sexual beings, Homo sexualis. We think: we are Homo sapiens. We also make tools and create new things: we are Homo faber, the makers. Using tools to understand and transform the world is one of our most distinctive characteristics. Technology is a basic fact of human life, woven into its very substance. Without technological innovation, the human civilization we know would not exist.

It undoubtedly takes courage to address questions of such scope. Professor Dong makes imaginative use of Maslow’s theory of needs, treating needs and desires as important drivers of human innovation. This thread runs throughout the book. Maslow arranged human needs in ascending order: physiological needs, safety, social belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. In his later work, he also explored self-transcendence. Within this framework, technological innovation can be understood as humanity’s continuing effort to meet one or more needs.

The main body of the book broadly follows these levels of need, using needs as a clear thread connecting the history of technological innovation from the earliest times. Admittedly, assigning each innovation to a particular level inevitably leaves room for differences of judgment. Yet the classification is no arbitrary assemblage. It embodies the author’s sustained reflection on the relationship between innovation and human society. The Database of Major Scientific and Technological Achievements alone testifies to the effort she has invested. She writes with thought and feeling as well as with evidence and data.

Reading this manuscript has been a pleasure, often accompanied by moments of surprise. Professor Dong has a gift for observing and experiencing the history of invention from the perspective of those who use technologies, giving a broad historical narrative a vivid sense of everyday life. In “The Inventive World of Food Lovers,” for example, she connects innovations spanning ten thousand years and many different fields: agriculture, ceramics, food processing, cooking, alcoholic drinks, soy sauce, cheese, and vitamin C. Together, they form a historical panorama of innovation by countless people. In “The Cheap Revolution and the Secrets of GDP Growth,” she examines how new products stimulate or even create material wants, and how standardized production reduces costs and prices, bringing products into wider use and driving the growth of wealth. This line of inquiry connects innovations in product combinations, production management, electrification, standardization, and automation, which together form a technological picture of the Industrial Revolution.

In this narrative, inventions become material building blocks through which we can understand the history of civilization. The history of technology unfolds across scientific, social, cultural, and psychological dimensions. Readers see each invention’s place within a network of human needs and its connections with institutions, ideas, and ways of life, as well as its internal workings. This is one of the book’s most illuminating qualities. Beyond the flashes of inspiration of a few exceptional individuals, it brings into view the historical process through which countless people and groups have responded to the needs of survival, social aspirations, and spiritual pursuits.

Another valuable quality is the author’s willingness to revise her understanding through discussion. Scholars have long disagreed about the relationships among magic, religion, and science, whether science existed in ancient China, and why modern science did not emerge there. Professor Dong listens carefully to different views, studies the relevant literature, and reaches her own judgments. She has moved beyond treating magic simply as science’s opposite or as “superstition.” She understands it as a practice through which early humans sought to comprehend and respond to the world within particular worldviews, modes of thought, and systems of value. Her engagement with the work of anthropologists such as Frazer, Malinowski, and Evans-Pritchard reveals a scholarly disposition open to self-criticism, revision, and the continuing expansion of intellectual horizons.

Publication of the second Chinese edition and the first English edition in the United States has a further significance. Scientific and technological innovation has always developed through exchanges, competition, borrowing, and adaptation among societies. Telling a global history of technological innovation through human needs can help readers from different cultural backgrounds move beyond familiar geographical and disciplinary boundaries. They can recognize what human experiences share while also seeing how civilizations differ in their selection, use, and evaluation of technologies. At a time when science and technology are increasingly global and the world faces risks of division, this kind of cross-cultural historical writing deserves particular attention.

The book discusses an enormous range of innovations and inventions, covering almost every aspect of human need. No author could offer a final judgment on every invention across so long a history, nor is that the purpose here. The book is more like a thought experiment on a grand scale. With the theory of needs as its warp and technological innovation as its weft, it seeks to weave an overall picture of the evolution of human civilization. History offers no ready-made trajectory. Researchers give it an intelligible structure through evidence, questions, and theories, and that work must continue as new materials, new questions, and the experiences of new eras emerge. The revised Chinese edition and its first English translation mark a new beginning in this intellectual journey. I congratulate Professor Dong and am pleased once again to recommend this courageous, insightful, and creative work to readers in both Chinese and English.
